Q: What is the CAP theorem and why is it fundamental to distributed systems design?
A: The CAP theorem, formulated by Eric Brewer, states that a distributed system can only guarantee two of three properties simultaneously: Consistency (all nodes see the same data at the same time), Availability (every request receives a response), and Partition tolerance (the system continues operating despite network failures). Since network partitions are inevitable in distributed systems, architects must choose between consistency and availability during failures. This theorem fundamentally shapes how distributed databases and systems are designed, leading to classifications like CP systems (prioritizing consistency) and AP systems (prioritizing availability).

Q: How does the Raft consensus algorithm achieve distributed consensus, and what are its key components?
A: Raft achieves consensus through leader election, log replication, and safety guarantees, designed to be more understandable than Paxos. The algorithm elects a single leader that handles all client requests and replicates log entries to follower nodes; followers only respond to the leader's requests. Raft uses randomized election timeouts to prevent split votes, and a leader must receive acknowledgment from a majority of nodes before committing entries. The algorithm guarantees that committed entries are durable and will eventually be applied by all nodes, ensuring linearizable semantics even during leader changes.

Q: What is eventual consistency and in what scenarios is it an appropriate choice?
A: Eventual consistency is a consistency model where, given no new updates, all replicas will eventually converge to the same value, though reads may temporarily return stale data. This model is appropriate for systems where high availability and low latency are more critical than immediate consistency, such as social media feeds, shopping carts, or DNS systems. Eventual consistency enables better partition tolerance and horizontal scalability since nodes can accept writes independently without coordination. Systems using this model often implement conflict resolution strategies like last-writer-wins, vector clocks, or application-specific merge functions to handle concurrent updates.

Q: What are the main strategies for data partitioning in distributed databases?
A: Data partitioning (or sharding) divides data across multiple nodes using strategies like range partitioning, hash partitioning, or directory-based partitioning. Range partitioning assigns contiguous key ranges to different nodes, enabling efficient range queries but risking hot spots if access patterns are skewed. Hash partitioning distributes data based on hash values of keys, providing better load distribution but making range queries inefficient since related data may be scattered. Consistent hashing is a popular variant that minimizes data movement when nodes are added or removed by mapping both nodes and keys to a ring structure.

Q: How does master-slave replication differ from multi-master replication, and what are the trade-offs?
A: Master-slave replication designates one node as the primary writer while replicas handle read requests, simplifying consistency but creating a single point of failure and write bottleneck. Multi-master replication allows writes on any node, improving write availability and geographic distribution but introducing conflict resolution complexity when concurrent writes occur. Master-slave systems offer stronger consistency guarantees since all writes go through one node, while multi-master systems sacrifice some consistency for better availability and lower write latency. The choice depends on workload patterns: read-heavy applications benefit from master-slave, while geographically distributed systems with write requirements may need multi-master.

Q: What is leader election and how do distributed systems typically implement it?
A: Leader election is the process of designating a single node to coordinate activities in a distributed system, essential for consensus protocols and managing shared resources. Common algorithms include the Bully algorithm (highest ID wins), Ring algorithm (token passing), and consensus-based approaches like those used in Raft and ZooKeeper. Leader election must handle network partitions carefully to prevent split-brain scenarios where multiple nodes believe they are the leader simultaneously. Modern systems often use lease-based mechanisms where leaders must periodically renew their leadership, automatically transferring authority if the leader fails to respond.

Q: What are distributed transactions and how does the two-phase commit protocol work?
A: Distributed transactions ensure atomicity across multiple nodes, guaranteeing that either all participants commit changes or all abort, maintaining data consistency across the system. The two-phase commit (2PC) protocol works in two phases: during the prepare phase, the coordinator asks all participants if they can commit, and in the commit phase, if all vote yes, the coordinator instructs everyone to commit. 2PC has significant drawbacks including blocking behavior if the coordinator fails after the prepare phase, and participants must hold locks until resolution. Alternatives like three-phase commit, Saga patterns, and consensus-based approaches like those in Spanner address some limitations but introduce their own trade-offs.

Q: How do message queues contribute to building resilient distributed systems?
A: Message queues decouple producers and consumers, allowing components to operate independently and at different speeds, which improves system resilience and scalability. They provide buffering during traffic spikes, preventing downstream services from being overwhelmed, and enable graceful degradation when components fail. Message queues support various delivery guarantees: at-most-once (messages may be lost), at-least-once (messages may be duplicated), and exactly-once (complex to achieve, often requiring idempotency). Popular implementations like Apache Kafka, RabbitMQ, and Amazon SQS offer different trade-offs between throughput, durability, ordering guarantees, and operational complexity.

Q: What fault tolerance patterns are essential for distributed systems reliability?
A: Essential fault tolerance patterns include redundancy (replicating data and services), circuit breakers (preventing cascade failures by failing fast), and bulkheads (isolating failures to prevent system-wide impact). Retry mechanisms with exponential backoff handle transient failures gracefully, while timeouts prevent indefinite blocking on unresponsive services. Health checks and heartbeats enable quick detection of failed nodes, allowing traffic to be redirected to healthy instances. Chaos engineering practices, popularized by Netflix's Chaos Monkey, proactively test fault tolerance by deliberately introducing failures in production systems.

Q: What is distributed caching and what challenges does it present?
A: Distributed caching stores frequently accessed data across multiple nodes to reduce database load and improve response times, with systems like Redis Cluster, Memcached, and Hazelcast being popular choices. Key challenges include cache invalidation (determining when cached data is stale), consistency between cache and source of truth, and handling cache stampedes when many requests simultaneously miss the cache. Cache partitioning strategies must balance load distribution with minimizing cross-node lookups, while replication provides fault tolerance at the cost of consistency complexity. Write-through, write-behind, and cache-aside patterns offer different trade-offs between consistency, performance, and implementation complexity.

Q: How does consistent hashing solve the problem of adding or removing nodes in a distributed system?
A: Consistent hashing maps both data keys and nodes to positions on a virtual ring, assigning each key to the first node encountered when moving clockwise from the key's position. When a node is added or removed, only keys that were mapped to that node need to be redistributed, minimizing data movement compared to traditional hash-based partitioning. Virtual nodes (vnodes) improve load distribution by assigning multiple positions on the ring to each physical node, reducing variance in data distribution. This technique is fundamental to systems like DynamoDB, Cassandra, and content delivery networks where frequent scaling operations must not disrupt the entire system.

Q: What is the split-brain problem and how do distributed systems prevent it?
A: Split-brain occurs when network partitions cause different parts of a distributed system to operate independently, potentially leading to conflicting decisions and data inconsistency. Prevention strategies include quorum-based voting (requiring a majority of nodes to agree), fencing tokens (invalidating old leaders), and STONITH (Shoot The Other Node In The Head) mechanisms that forcibly terminate potentially conflicting nodes. Systems often use odd numbers of nodes and require majority agreement to ensure only one partition can make progress during a split. Proper split-brain handling is critical for maintaining data integrity in systems like distributed databases, cluster managers, and coordination services.

Q: What are vector clocks and how do they track causality in distributed systems?
A: Vector clocks are a mechanism for tracking causality and detecting concurrent events in distributed systems, where each node maintains a vector of logical timestamps for all nodes. When a node sends a message or performs an operation, it increments its own counter and includes the entire vector; receivers update their vectors by taking the maximum of each component. Two events are concurrent if neither vector clock dominates the other, indicating potential conflicts that need resolution. Vector clocks enable systems like Amazon's Dynamo to detect conflicting writes and provide version history, though they can grow large in systems with many nodes.

Q: How do gossip protocols enable efficient information dissemination in large distributed systems?
A: Gossip protocols spread information through randomized peer-to-peer communication, where each node periodically shares its state with randomly selected neighbors who then propagate updates further. This approach provides eventual consistency with high probability, scalability to thousands of nodes, and resilience to node failures without requiring centralized coordination. Gossip is used for membership management, failure detection, and data synchronization in systems like Cassandra, Consul, and Serf. The protocol's probabilistic nature means information reaches all nodes in O(log N) rounds with high probability, making it efficient for large clusters while tolerating message loss and node failures.

Q: What is the Saga pattern and when should it be used instead of distributed transactions?
A: The Saga pattern manages distributed transactions by breaking them into a sequence of local transactions, each with a compensating action that can undo its effects if later steps fail. Unlike two-phase commit, Sagas do not require distributed locks and can span long-running operations across multiple services, making them suitable for microservices architectures. There are two coordination approaches: choreography (services react to events) and orchestration (a central coordinator directs the flow). Sagas trade strong consistency for availability and are appropriate when business processes can tolerate temporary inconsistency and when operations are naturally reversible or compensatable.
