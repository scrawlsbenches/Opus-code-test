Q: What is the fundamental difference between a mutex and a semaphore, and when would you choose one over the other?
A: A mutex (mutual exclusion) is a binary lock that allows only one thread to access a critical section at a time, while a semaphore is a counting mechanism that permits a specified number of concurrent accesses. Mutexes enforce strict ownership where only the locking thread can unlock, whereas semaphores have no ownership concept and any thread can signal. Choose a mutex when you need exclusive access to a shared resource with clear ownership semantics, and use a semaphore when you need to limit concurrent access to a pool of resources or coordinate between multiple producers and consumers.

Q: How does the producer-consumer pattern work, and what synchronization primitives are typically used to implement it?
A: The producer-consumer pattern involves one or more producer threads generating data items and placing them into a shared buffer, while consumer threads remove and process these items concurrently. Implementation typically requires a bounded buffer protected by a mutex, along with two condition variables or semaphores: one to signal when the buffer has available space for producers, and another to signal when items are available for consumers. This pattern decouples data production from consumption, allowing both to proceed at their own rates while the buffer absorbs temporary imbalances in processing speeds.

Q: What problem do reader-writer locks solve, and how do they differ from standard mutexes?
A: Reader-writer locks address the asymmetry between read and write operations on shared data, where multiple concurrent reads are safe but writes require exclusive access. Unlike a standard mutex that blocks all concurrent access, a reader-writer lock allows unlimited simultaneous readers while ensuring writers get exclusive access when modifying data. This pattern significantly improves throughput in read-heavy workloads, though implementations must guard against writer starvation by occasionally prioritizing waiting writers over new readers.

Q: What are the key benefits of using a thread pool, and what considerations affect optimal pool sizing?
A: Thread pools maintain a collection of reusable worker threads that execute submitted tasks, eliminating the overhead of repeatedly creating and destroying threads for short-lived work. Benefits include reduced latency for task execution, bounded resource consumption, and simplified thread lifecycle management. Optimal pool size depends on the nature of tasks: CPU-bound work typically benefits from a pool sized to the number of processor cores, while I/O-bound tasks can use larger pools since threads spend time waiting rather than consuming CPU cycles.

Q: How do futures and promises enable asynchronous programming, and what is their relationship to each other?
A: A promise represents the producer side of an asynchronous computation where a value will eventually be provided, while a future represents the consumer side where that value can be retrieved once available. The promise is used by the code performing the computation to fulfill or reject the result, and the future is returned to calling code to await and retrieve that result. This separation allows asynchronous operations to be composed, chained, and coordinated without explicit thread management, enabling cleaner expression of concurrent workflows.

Q: What is the actor model, and how does it differ from shared-memory concurrency approaches?
A: The actor model treats actors as the fundamental unit of computation, where each actor encapsulates private state and communicates exclusively through asynchronous message passing with other actors. Unlike shared-memory approaches that require locks and synchronization primitives, actors eliminate data races by design since no state is ever shared directly between concurrent entities. Each actor processes messages sequentially from its mailbox, simplifying reasoning about correctness, and the model naturally scales across distributed systems since message passing works identically whether actors are local or remote.

Q: What is Communicating Sequential Processes (CSP), and how does it approach concurrent programming?
A: CSP is a formal model for describing concurrent systems where independent processes communicate through synchronized channels rather than shared memory. Processes block when sending to or receiving from a channel until a corresponding partner is ready, creating natural synchronization points that eliminate race conditions. Languages like Go implement CSP concepts through goroutines and channels, making it straightforward to express complex concurrent coordination patterns while maintaining clear data ownership and avoiding the pitfalls of lock-based synchronization.

Q: What makes a data structure lock-free, and what advantages does this provide over lock-based alternatives?
A: A lock-free data structure guarantees system-wide progress, meaning at least one thread will complete its operation in a finite number of steps, even if other threads are suspended or delayed. Unlike lock-based structures where a thread holding a lock can block all others, lock-free designs use atomic compare-and-swap operations to make progress without mutual exclusion. This provides immunity to priority inversion, deadlock, and convoying, while often delivering better performance under high contention, though the implementation complexity is significantly higher.

Q: What are atomic operations, and why are they essential for correct concurrent programming?
A: Atomic operations are indivisible actions that complete entirely without interruption, appearing to other threads as happening instantaneously at a single point in time. They are essential because non-atomic operations on shared variables can be interleaved with operations from other threads, leading to torn reads, lost updates, and other race conditions. Modern processors provide hardware support for atomic instructions like compare-and-swap, fetch-and-add, and atomic loads/stores, which serve as building blocks for lock-free algorithms and synchronization primitives.

Q: What is a race condition, and how can it manifest in concurrent programs?
A: A race condition occurs when program behavior depends on the relative timing of operations across multiple threads accessing shared state, producing different results based on unpredictable scheduling decisions. Common manifestations include check-then-act races where a condition becomes invalid between testing and action, read-modify-write races where updates are lost when threads interleave, and initialization races where objects are accessed before being fully constructed. Race conditions are particularly insidious because they may only manifest under specific timing conditions, making them difficult to reproduce and debug during development.

Q: What is memory ordering, and why do modern concurrent programs need to consider it?
A: Memory ordering refers to the guarantees about when memory operations performed by one thread become visible to other threads, which may differ from the program order due to compiler and hardware optimizations. Modern processors reorder instructions and buffer writes for performance, meaning a write on one core may not be immediately visible to reads on another core without explicit synchronization. Concurrent programs must use memory fences, atomic operations with appropriate ordering constraints, or higher-level synchronization primitives to ensure correct visibility of shared data across threads.

Q: How does the compare-and-swap (CAS) operation work, and what role does it play in lock-free programming?
A: Compare-and-swap atomically compares a memory location's current value against an expected value and, only if they match, replaces it with a new value, returning whether the operation succeeded. This primitive enables optimistic concurrency control where threads attempt updates without locking, detect conflicts through CAS failures, and retry with updated values. CAS forms the foundation of most lock-free algorithms and many synchronization primitives, allowing threads to coordinate without blocking each other, though care must be taken to handle the ABA problem where a value changes and returns to its original state between reads.

Q: What is the difference between sequential consistency and acquire-release memory ordering?
A: Sequential consistency provides the strongest guarantees, ensuring all threads observe memory operations in a single total order consistent with each thread's program order, effectively behaving as if operations were interleaved on a single processor. Acquire-release ordering is weaker, guaranteeing only that operations before a release are visible to operations after a corresponding acquire on the same synchronization variable. Sequential consistency is easier to reason about but more expensive, while acquire-release provides sufficient guarantees for most synchronization patterns with better performance on weakly-ordered architectures.

Q: What strategies can prevent deadlock in concurrent systems?
A: Deadlock prevention typically employs one of several strategies to break the four necessary conditions: mutual exclusion, hold-and-wait, no preemption, and circular wait. The most practical approach is preventing circular wait by establishing a global ordering on locks and always acquiring them in that order. Other strategies include using timeouts with trylock operations to detect and recover from potential deadlocks, designing lock-free alternatives that eliminate blocking entirely, or using single-lock designs that serialize all access through one synchronization point at the cost of concurrency.

Q: How do condition variables enable efficient thread coordination, and what is the spurious wakeup problem?
A: Condition variables allow threads to efficiently wait for specific conditions by releasing an associated mutex and sleeping until signaled, avoiding wasteful busy-waiting while maintaining correctness. When signaled, a waiting thread reacquires the mutex before proceeding, ensuring the condition can be safely rechecked. The spurious wakeup problem occurs because implementations may wake threads without an explicit signal, so waiting must always be performed in a loop that rechecks the condition, as the condition may not actually hold when the thread resumes execution.
