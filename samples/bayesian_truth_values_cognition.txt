Bayesian Truth Values in Cognitive Reasoning

Binary logic assigns propositions the value true or false, but intelligent systems operating in uncertain environments require a richer representation. Real-world knowledge is rarely certain. A cognitive agent must distinguish between what it strongly believes with little evidence and what it weakly believes with overwhelming evidence. Bayesian truth values provide this expressiveness by treating belief as a probability distribution rather than a discrete state.

A Bayesian truth value consists of two components: strength and confidence. Strength represents the probability that a proposition is true, ranging continuously from zero to one. Confidence represents the quantity of evidence supporting that strength estimate. Two beliefs may share identical strength yet differ dramatically in confidence. A belief based on a thousand observations carries more weight than one based on two observations, even if both suggest seventy percent probability. This dual representation captures the essential difference between ignorance and knowledge.

The Beta distribution elegantly models this uncertainty. Parameterized by alpha and beta counts representing positive and negative evidence, the Beta distribution's mean gives the strength while its concentration (alpha plus beta) reflects confidence. A Beta(7,3) distribution indicates seven positive observations, three negative, yielding strength 0.7 with moderate confidence. A Beta(70,30) shares the same strength but with ten times the confidence, producing a much narrower distribution around that estimate. The variance of the Beta distribution decreases as evidence accumulates, reflecting increasing certainty.

Bayesian updating describes how beliefs change when new observations arrive. Upon observing positive evidence, alpha increments; negative evidence increments beta. The posterior distribution combines prior belief with new data, automatically weighting recent evidence appropriately. A highly confident prior resists change from small amounts of contradictory evidence, while an uncertain prior shifts rapidly. This update rule is mathematically optimal under the assumption of independent observations. The elegance of conjugate priors ensures that updates remain computationally tractable.

When merging evidence from multiple sources, the agent combines Beta distributions by summing their pseudo-counts. If Source A contributes Beta(10,5) and Source B contributes Beta(8,2), the merged belief becomes Beta(18,7). This assumes source independence; correlated sources require more sophisticated treatment to avoid double-counting evidence. Source reliability can be modeled by discounting contributions from unreliable sources.

The distinction between confidence and strength illuminates important cognitive phenomena. High confidence with low strength means the agent is certain something is probably false. Low confidence with high strength means the agent suspects something is probably true but lacks sufficient evidence. These states demand different responses: the former warrants dismissal, the latter warrants investigation. A cognitive agent that conflates these states will either waste resources investigating settled questions or prematurely dismiss promising hypotheses.

Surprise quantifies prediction error as the absolute difference between predicted and observed outcomes. When an agent predicts probability 0.9 and observes failure, surprise equals 0.9. When it predicts 0.5 and observes success, surprise equals only 0.5. Surprise serves as a learning signal, indicating where the agent's model deviates from reality. High surprise events are information-rich, revealing gaps in the agent's understanding.

Learning rate should be modulated by surprise. Surprising events carry more information than expected events. An outcome that violates confident predictions signals model inadequacy, warranting larger belief updates. Conversely, expected outcomes confirm existing beliefs, requiring minimal adjustment. This adaptive learning rate accelerates knowledge acquisition in novel domains while maintaining stability in well-understood territories. The principle mirrors biological learning, where dopamine signals scale with prediction error.

New knowledge enters the system with default truth values reflecting epistemic humility. A reasonable default is Beta(1,1), the uniform distribution expressing complete uncertainty. Alternatively, domain-specific priors may encode known base rates. The choice of default significantly affects early learning dynamics, gradually diminishing in influence as evidence accumulates. Jeffreys prior Beta(0.5,0.5) offers an alternative that is invariant under reparameterization.

Truth values connect intimately to attention allocation. Propositions with low confidence deserve investigative attention to gather more evidence. Propositions with high surprise warrant corrective attention to update models. Propositions with high strength and high confidence can be trusted for decision-making without further deliberation. This relationship enables the cognitive agent to allocate its limited processing resources efficiently, focusing on beliefs that most need refinement or that most strongly predict important outcomes. Attention thus becomes a resource allocation problem guided by epistemic value.

The integration of Bayesian truth values into cognitive architecture enables graceful degradation under uncertainty. Rather than failing when faced with incomplete information, the agent maintains calibrated beliefs that honestly represent its epistemic state. Decisions can incorporate uncertainty directly, choosing actions that are robust across the range of possible truths. This probabilistic foundation supports both cautious behavior when confidence is low and decisive action when evidence is strong.

Decay mechanisms prevent stale beliefs from dominating current reasoning. Evidence gathered long ago may no longer reflect present reality. Temporal discounting reduces the effective count of old observations, allowing recent evidence to exert proportionally greater influence. This creates a sliding window of relevance while preserving the mathematical elegance of the Beta distribution framework.

Threshold effects emerge when truth values cross decision boundaries. An agent may require strength above 0.7 with confidence above fifty observations before committing to action. These thresholds convert continuous beliefs into discrete decisions, bridging probabilistic reasoning with practical behavior. Calibration ensures that stated probabilities match empirical frequencies, enabling meaningful threshold selection.
